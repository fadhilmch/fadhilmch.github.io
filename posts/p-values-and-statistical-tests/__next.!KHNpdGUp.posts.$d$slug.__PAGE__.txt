34:T289b6,<p>You change the colour of a Buy button. With the old button, 1,000 out of 10,000 people buy. With the new one, 1,080 out of 10,000 do. The new button looks better.</p>
<p>But if you’d shown the <em>old</em> button to two different groups of 10,000 people, you wouldn’t have got the same number twice either. People are random. One week 1,000 buy, another week 1,040. So is the extra 80 the button, or just the usual noise?</p>
<p>That’s the question statistical tests answer, and p-values are how they report the answer. I’ve read the textbook definition of a p-value many times and it still slipped out of my head every time. So in this post I build it up from something small enough to count by hand, ten coin flips, and then take exactly the same idea back to the button.</p>
<p>If words like <em>distribution</em>, <em>standard deviation</em> or <em>normal curve</em> are new to you, my <a href="/posts/probability-and-distributions/">probability post</a> covers them from scratch. You can also just read on. I explain each one briefly when it first comes up.</p>

<h2>The idea in one paragraph</h2>
<p>Before any maths, here’s the whole trick. <strong>Assume nothing interesting is going on. Work out how often pure chance would give you a result at least as extreme as yours. If that’s rare, start doubting the “nothing is going on” story.</strong></p>
<p>It’s the same logic as a courtroom. The defendant is presumed innocent. The question the jury asks is: <em>if they really were innocent, how surprising would this evidence be?</em> Their fingerprints on the window: a bit surprising, but there could be an innocent reason. Fingerprints, CCTV footage and the stolen laptop in their car: so surprising that “innocent” stops being believable. A p-value is a number for exactly that: how surprising your data would be if nothing were going on.</p>
<p>Everything else in this post is detail around that one move.</p>
<h2>Ten coin flips</h2>
<p>A friend hands you a coin and says it’s fair. You flip it 10 times and get <strong>8 heads</strong>. Do you believe them?</p>
<h3>Step 1: assume the boring explanation</h3>
<p>The boring explanation is “the coin is fair”: a 50% chance of heads on every flip, and each flip ignores the ones before it. In statistics the boring explanation is called the <strong>null hypothesis</strong>, written <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>H</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">H_0</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8333em;vertical-align:-0.15em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0813em;">H</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.3011em;"><span style="top:-2.55em;margin-left:-0.0813em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight">0</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.15em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span>. “Null” as in no effect, nothing going on.</p>
<p>We’re not saying we believe it. We’re adopting it for a moment so we can ask a question we can actually answer: <em>what does a fair coin do?</em></p>
<h3>Step 2: watch what a fair coin does</h3>
<p>The quickest way to get a feel for it is to watch. Below, one “trial” is 10 flips of a fair coin. Each finished trial adds one to the bar for its number of heads. Run a few hundred.</p>

<p>A few things jump out. Five heads is the most common result, but it only happens about a quarter of the time. Four and six are close behind. Eight or more is rare, but not unheard of: a perfectly fair coin produces it every now and then.</p>
<p>We can also get the exact numbers instead of simulating. Each flip has 2 outcomes, so ten flips can come out in 2 × 2 × … × 2 = 2¹⁰ = 1,024 different orders. With a fair coin, every one of those orders is equally likely.</p>

<h3>Step 3: decide what “at least this extreme” means</h3>
<p>We got 8 heads, which is 3 away from the 5 a fair coin would give on average. So the question becomes: how often does a fair coin land <strong>3 or more away from 5</strong>? That includes 8, 9 and 10 heads, and also 2, 1 and 0. Two heads would be just as suspicious as eight. It’s the same distance from fair, just in the other direction.</p>

<p>Why not just ask how likely <em>exactly</em> 8 heads is? Because any single exact result is unlikely. Even the most common result, 5 heads, only happens a quarter of the time, and with a thousand flips every exact count would have a tiny chance. What we want to know is how far out towards the edges our result sits, so we count it together with everything even further out.</p>

<h3>Step 5: decide</h3>
<p>Is 11% surprising enough to say the coin isn’t fair? Something that happens about one time in nine isn’t very rare.</p>
<p>To avoid arguing about it after the fact, you pick a cut-off <em>before</em> looking at the data. The usual cut-off is 5%. It’s called the <strong>significance level</strong> and written <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0037em;">α</span></span></span></span> (alpha). If p comes out below <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0037em;">α</span></span></span></span>, the result is called <strong>statistically significant</strong> and you reject the null hypothesis.</p>
<p>Our 10.9% is above 5%, so we don’t reject “fair”. Be careful with what that means, though. It does <strong>not</strong> prove the coin is fair. Ten flips is very little evidence, and a coin that lands heads 70% of the time would often give results like ours too. Not significant means “not enough evidence”, which is a very different statement from “no effect”.</p>
<p>The 5% isn’t magic either. Ronald Fisher suggested it in the 1920s as a convenient line, and it stuck. A p-value of 4.9% and one of 5.1% are practically the same evidence, even though one gets called significant and the other doesn’t.</p>
<h3>One-sided or two-sided</h3>
<p>We counted both edges because a coin biased either way would be a problem. That’s a <strong>two-sided test</strong>. If, <em>before flipping</em>, you only cared whether the coin favours heads, you’d count only 8, 9 and 10 and get half the p-value, 5.5%. That’s a <strong>one-sided test</strong>.</p>
<p>The rule is to choose before you see the data. Choosing the side afterwards because it gives the smaller number is cheating, even if it doesn’t feel like it.</p>

class="mrel">≤</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">2</span><span class="mclose">)</span></span></span></span></span>
<p>“8 or more, plus 2 or fewer”. That’s the 112/1024 you already worked out, written shorter.</p>
<h2>What a p-value is, and what it isn’t</h2>
<p>Here’s the general version of what we just did:</p>
<span class="katex-display"><span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>P</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mtext>a result at least this extreme</mtext><mo>∣</mo><msub><mi>H</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p = P(\text{a result at least this extreme} \mid H_0)</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.625em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">p</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.1389em;">P</span><span class="mopen">(</span><span class="mord text"><span class="mord">a result at least this extreme</span></span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">∣</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0813em;">H</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.3011em;"><span style="top:-2.55em;margin-left:-0.0813em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight">0</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.15em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span><span class="mclose">)</span></span></span></span></span>
<p>Read the bar as “assuming”: <em>the chance of a result at least this extreme, assuming the null hypothesis is true.</em></p>
<p>That “assuming” is where almost everyone goes wrong, me included for a long time. The p-value <em>starts</em> by assuming <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>H</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">H_0</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8333em;vertical-align:-0.15em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0813em;">H</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.3011em;"><span style="top:-2.55em;margin-left:-0.0813em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight">0</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.15em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span> is true, so it can’t then tell you the chance that <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>H</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">H_0</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8333em;vertical-align:-0.15em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0813em;">H</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.3011em;"><span style="top:-2.55em;margin-left:-0.0813em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight">0</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.15em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span> is true. A p-value of 3% does <strong>not</strong> mean:</p>
<ul>
<li>a 3% chance there’s no real effect,</li>
<li>a 3% chance the result is a fluke,</li>
<li>a 97% chance your change works.</li>
</ul>
<p>It means: if there were no real effect, results this extreme would turn up 3% of the time.</p>
<p>That can sound like hair-splitting, so here’s a case where the difference is obvious. A smoke alarm rarely goes off when there’s no fire: say 1% of the time. That’s like a p-value of 1%. Does it mean that when the alarm goes off, there’s a 99% chance of fire? Not in my kitchen. If you burn toast most mornings, most alarms are toast. “How often does it ring when there’s no fire?” and “now that it’s ringing, how likely is a fire?” are different questions. The p-value only answers the first one. (If you read the probability post, this is the “order matters when you say <em>given</em>” trap.)</p>

<p>Any rule that makes decisions from noisy data will sometimes get it wrong. There are exactly two ways it can happen:</p>

<ul>
<li>A <strong>false positive</strong> (also called a Type I error) is declaring an effect that isn’t there. If there’s really nothing going on, this happens with chance <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0037em;">α</span></span></span></span>. At 5%, about 1 in 20 tests of useless changes will still come out “significant”.</li>
<li>A <strong>false negative</strong> (a Type II error) is missing an effect that is there. Its chance is written <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>β</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\beta</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8889em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0528em;">β</span></span></span></span> (beta).</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Power</strong> is the other side of a false negative: the chance your test catches a real effect of a given size.</p>
<span class="katex-display"><span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mtext>power</mtext><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mi>β</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\text{power} = 1 - \beta</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.625em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord text"><span class="mord">power</span></span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.7278em;vertical-align:-0.0833em;"></span><span class="mord">1</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span><span class="mbin">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8889em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0528em;">β</span></span></span></span></span>
<p>Power depends on how big the real effect is and how much data you have. Big effects are easy to spot. Small ones need a lot of data. We’ll work it out for the button shortly.</p>
<h2>Back to the button</h2>
<p>Here’s the button experiment. Visitors were randomly split into two groups, often called <strong>arms</strong>: group A saw the old button, group B the new one.</p>
<p>| Group | Visitors | Bought | Conversion rate |
|—|—:|—:|—:|
| A: old button | 10,000 | 1,000 | 10.0% |
| B: new button | 10,000 | 1,080 | 10.8% |</p>
<p>B converts <strong>0.8 percentage points</strong> better: 10.8% against 10.0%. As a relative change, that’s 8% better, because 0.8 is 8% of 10. Both are correct. Just be clear which one you mean, because people mix them up all the time.</p>
<p>The null hypothesis is that the button makes no difference: both groups have the same true conversion rate, and the 0.8-point gap is noise. We’ll use a two-sided test with <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>5</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">%</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha = 5\%</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0037em;">α</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8056em;vertical-align:-0.0556em;"></span><span class="mord">5%</span></span></span></span>, decided before the experiment started.</p>
<p>With the coin we could list every possible outcome. With 20,000 people that’s hopeless. But there’s a neat way to build the “nothing going on” world anyway.</p>
<h3>Shuffle the labels</h3>
<p>If the button truly makes no difference, then the labels A and B are meaningless. Someone who bought would have bought with either button, and someone who didn’t, wouldn’t have. So:</p>
<ol>
<li>Take all 20,000 people with their outcomes: 2,080 bought, 17,920 didn’t.</li>
<li>Throw the A and B labels away, shuffle, and deal everyone into two new groups of 10,000.</li>
<li>Work out the difference in conversion rate between the two new groups.</li>
<li>Repeat thousands of times.</li>
</ol>

The pile of shuffled differences is the “nothing going on” world, just like the coin chart was. Then you count: how often is a shuffled difference at least as far from zero as our real +0.8?</p>

<p>After a few thousand shuffles you’ll settle at around 6.7%. That’s the p-value. This method is called a <strong>permutation test</strong>, and I like it a lot, because there’s no formula hiding anything. It’s the coin’s count-and-divide, done by a computer. Here it is in Python:</p>
<pre><code class="hljs language-python"><span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> numpy <span class="hljs-keyword">as</span> np

rng = np.random.default_rng(<span class="hljs-number">0</span>)
a = np.r_[np.ones(<span class="hljs-number">1000</span>), np.zeros(<span class="hljs-number">9000</span>)]   <span class="hljs-comment"># old button: 1 = bought</span>
b = np.r_[np.ones(<span class="hljs-number">1080</span>), np.zeros(<span class="hljs-number">8920</span>)]   <span class="hljs-comment"># new button</span>
observed = b.mean() - a.mean()             <span class="hljs-comment"># 0.008</span>
everyone = np.concatenate([a, b])

shuffles, extreme = <span class="hljs-number">10_000</span>, <span class="hljs-number">0</span>
<span class="hljs-keyword">for</span> _ <span class="hljs-keyword">in</span> <span class="hljs-built_in">range</span>(shuffles):
    rng.shuffle(everyone)
    diff = everyone[<span class="hljs-number">10_000</span>:].mean() - everyone[:<span class="hljs-number">10_000</span>].mean()
    extreme += <span class="hljs-built_in">abs</span>(diff) >= <span class="hljs-built_in">abs</span>(observed) - <span class="hljs-number">1e-12</span>   <span class="hljs-comment"># tiny tolerance for rounding</span>

p = (extreme + <span class="hljs-number">1</span>) / (shuffles + <span class="hljs-number">1</span>)   <span class="hljs-comment"># count the real split as one of the shuffles</span>
<span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(p)  <span class="hljs-comment"># about 0.067; it moves a little from run to run</span>
</code></pre>
<p>One caution: shuffling only works when people were randomly assigned and are independent of each other. If the same person shows up several times, or whole households were assigned together, you have to shuffle those units together, or the “nothing going on” world you build is wrong.</p>
<h3>The shortcut: the z-test</h3>
<p>Shuffling works, but before computers it was impossible, and on huge datasets it’s still slow. Look at the shape the shuffled differences made, though: a bell. That’s the central limit theorem at work. A conversion rate is an average of lots of 0s and 1s, and averages come out bell-shaped.</p>
<p>If we know the null world is a bell centred on zero, we only need one more number to draw it: how wide it is. That width is the <strong>standard error</strong> (SE), the amount the difference between two groups typically wobbles from random sampling alone. Then we measure our result in “standard errors away from zero” and read the answer off the bell. That’s the <strong>two-proportion z-test</strong>. One step at a time:</p>
<p><strong>1. Find the shared conversion rate.</strong> Under the null, both groups have the same rate, so pool them: 2,080 buyers out of 20,000.</p>

<p>The hat means “estimated from data”. Annoyingly, this <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mover accent="true"><mi>p</mi><mo>^</mo></mover></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\hat p</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8889em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord accent"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.6944em;"><span style="top:-3em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:3em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">p</span></span><span style="top:-3em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:3em;"></span><span class="accent-body" style="left:-0.1667em;"><span class="mord">^</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.1944em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span> is a conversion rate, not a p-value. Same letter, different job.</p>

<li>Double it for the left tail: 0.064.</li>
</ul>
<p>So <strong>p ≈ 6.4%</strong>, very close to the 6.7% from shuffling. Two completely different methods, nearly the same answer. (The small gap is because the bell is a smooth approximation of what are really lumpy, whole-number counts.) Tick the overlay box in the shuffle widget and you’ll see the bell sitting right on top of the shuffled bars.</p>
<p><strong>5. Decide.</strong> 6.4% is above our 5% line, so the result is not significant. We haven’t shown the new button is better. We also haven’t shown it isn’t. The dashed lines at ±1.96 in the chart are where the 5% cut-off sits: <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>z</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">z</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.044em;">z</span></span></span></span> has to get past 1.96 to count as significant, and ours stopped at 1.85.</p>
<p>You won’t do this by hand in practice. In Python it’s one line:</p>

<h2>Same effect, more data</h2>
<p>Now imagine we’d run the test with 20,000 people per group instead, and got exactly the same rates, 10.0% and 10.8%.</p>
<p>The effect is the same. But the standard error shrinks with more data, by a square root: double the people and the wobble gets divided by √2 ≈ 1.41.</p>

<p>Now it’s significant. Nothing about the button changed. We just measured it more precisely. This is worth sitting with for a second: <strong>a p-value mixes up how big an effect is with how much data you collected.</strong> With enough users, a tiny, useless effect becomes “significant”. With too few, a big, valuable one doesn’t.</p>
<p>That’s a comparison between two separate experiments, by the way. It is <em>not</em> permission to keep adding users to a finished test until it crosses the line. More on that in the traps section.</p>
<h2>Power: how many users do you need?</h2>
<p>Before you run the experiment, ask: if the new button really does add 0.8 points, how likely is this test to notice?</p>
<p>With 10,000 people per group, the answer is only about <strong>46%</strong>. That’s worse than a coin flip. You would miss a real, worthwhile improvement more often than you’d find it. With 20,000 per group it’s about 75%.</p>
<p>The widget shows why. Imagine running the same experiment many times. The blue curve is where your measured lift would land if the button did nothing. The purple curve is where it would land if the true lift really is +0.8 points. Anything outside the dashed lines counts as significant. Power is the share of the purple curve that ends up outside them.</p>

<p>Power is a planning tool. After the experiment, working out “observed power” from your own result doesn’t tell you anything the p-value hasn’t already. Look at the confidence interval instead.</p>
<h2>Confidence intervals: how big is the effect?</h2>
<p>A p-value tells you whether a result is surprising. It doesn’t tell you how big the effect is, and that’s usually what you actually want to know. For that, use a <strong>confidence interval</strong>: your estimate, plus or minus a margin for the wobble.</p>
<span class="katex-display"><span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mtext>interval</mtext><mo>=</mo><mo <p>What does the “95%” mean? It’s about the method, not this one interval. If you repeated the experiment many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true difference.</p>

<p>Before you run a test, decide the smallest lift that would be worth the work, then compare the interval against <em>that</em> line, not just against zero. Statistically significant and worth doing are different questions.</p>
<h2>Three ways to fool yourself</h2>
<h3>1. Testing lots of things at once</h3>
<p>Say you check 20 metrics on a button that changes nothing. Each test has a 5% chance of a false alarm. The chance that <em>at least one</em> of them comes out significant, if the tests are independent, is:</p>
<span class="katex-display"><span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mn>0.05</mn><msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mn>20</mn></msup><mo>≈</mo><mn>64</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">%</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">1 - (1 - 0.05)^{20} \approx 64\%</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.7278em;vertical-align:-0.0833em;"></span><span class="mord">1</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span><span class="mbin">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mopen">(</span><span class="mord">1</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span><span class="mbin">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1.1141em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">0.05</span><span class="mclose"><span class="mclose">)</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.8641em;"><span style="top:-3.113em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight"><span class="mord mtight">20</span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">≈</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8056em;vertical-align:-0.0556em;"></span><span class="mord">64%</span></span></span></span></span>
<p>Each test has a 95% chance of staying quiet. All 20 staying quiet is 0.95 multiplied by itself 20 times, about 36%. Everything else, 64%, is at least one false alarm. Look at enough metrics and something will always light up.</p>

<p>The fix: pick one primary metric before the experiment. If you must make claims about many tests, tighten the cut-off. The simplest way is the <strong>Bonferroni correction</strong>: divide <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0037em;">α</span></span></span></span> by the number of tests, so 0.05 / 20 = 0.0025 each. The <strong>Benjamini-Hochberg</strong> procedure is less strict and controls the share of your “discoveries” that are false, rather than the chance of any false alarm at all.</p>
<h3>2. Peeking</h3>
<p>You plan a two-week test. On day 3 you check: p = 0.04! You stop and ship.</p>
<p>The trouble is that the 5% false alarm rate only holds if you look once, at the end. Every peek is another chance for noise to wander across the line, and noise wanders a lot early on, when there’s little data. In a quick simulation of a button that does nothing, checked 20 times along the way and stopped at the first p below 0.05, about <strong>a quarter</strong> of the experiments ended in a false “win”.</p>
<p>Either decide the sample size in advance and look once, or use a method built for repeated looks (sequential testing). An ordinary p-value doesn’t protect you from peeking.</p>
<h3>3. Reading “not significant” as “no effect”</h3>
<p>Back to the button: estimated +0.80 points, interval −0.05 to +1.65, p ≈ 0.064. The data leave room for no improvement <em>and</em> for a useful one. “Not significant” just means this experiment couldn’t tell. If you need to show that two things are effectively the same, that’s a different test (an equivalence test), with its own planning.</p>
<h2>Choosing a test</h2>
<p>The coin and the button used tests for counts and rates. Other data needs other tests, but they mostly share the same shape: <strong>(the difference you saw) / (how much it would wobble by chance)</strong>, compared against what that ratio looks like when nothing is going on.</p>
<p>Two questions get you most of the way to the right one:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>What is one observation?</strong> One independent user? The same person measured twice? A whole household? Getting this wrong breaks most tests.</li>
<li><strong>What are you measuring?</strong> A yes/no outcome gives you a rate. Revenue or time on page gives you numbers with an average.</li>
</ol>
<p>| Situation | Common test | Watch out for |
|—|—|—|
| Two independent conversion rates | Two-proportion z-test | Very small counts need an exact test |
| Two independent averages | Welch’s t-test | Big outliers, such as a few huge orders |
| The same people measured twice | Paired t-test | Test the per-person differences |
| Two groups, skewed numbers | Mann-Whitney U test | It compares rankings, not averages |
| Three or more groups | ANOVA, or similar | Then adjust for multiple comparisons |
| Randomised groups you can reshuffle | Permutation test | Shuffle the unit that was randomised |</p>

Difference on top, wobble underneath, same as before. The result is compared against a <strong>t-distribution</strong>, which is a bell with slightly fatter tails to account for the extra uncertainty of small samples.</p>

<h2>The checklist</h2>
<p>When I read or run a test now, I go through it in this order:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>What’s the boring explanation?</strong> Write down the null hypothesis, and decide one- or two-sided, before looking at the data.</li>
<li><strong>How big is the difference?</strong> Keep the units: +0.8 percentage points, not just “significant”.</li>
<li><strong>How much could it wobble by chance?</strong> Check that observations are independent and the standard error makes sense.</li>
<li><strong>How surprising is it if nothing is going on?</strong> That’s the p-value, and it is <em>not</em> the chance the null is true.</li>
<li><strong>What rule did we agree on?</strong> Compare against <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\alpha</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0037em;">α</span></span></span></span> without moving the goalposts, switching metrics or stopping early.</li>
<li><strong>What would we actually do?</strong> Compare the confidence interval with the smallest lift worth shipping, and plan the next test’s sample size for an effect that matters.</li>
</ol>
<p>The coin is the whole idea in miniature: assume fair, count how often fair looks this lopsided, add up the blue bars. Every test after that is the same question with a different way of counting.</p>
