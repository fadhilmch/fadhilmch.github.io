1d:T54ff,<p>A grayscale photograph still tells us a lot. We can see a person, a tree, a patch of sky. But a shirt that looks dark might have been blue, green or red. Removing colour throws away information. So putting colour back is a prediction. There’s nothing to undo.</p>
<p>In 2019, I built an image colorization model for the DD2424 course at KTH. I used TensorFlow and Keras to reproduce the approach in <em>Deep Koalarization</em>, with some modifications. The model combined a convolutional encoder with a pretrained Inception-ResNet-v2 network, then decoded their combined features into colour.</p>
<p>The interesting part is how those two paths help each other. One looks at local detail. The other provides context for the whole image. Neither can tell us the original colour of every object.</p>
<figure class="fig color-fig">
<img src="/assets/images/projects/colorization-example.svg" alt="A landscape example from my project: grayscale input on the left, predicted colour in the middle, original colour on the right. The sky becomes blue, while the tree and vegetation do not fully match the original.">
<figcaption>Actual landscape output from my 2,000-image experiment. Left: grayscale. Middle: prediction. Right: original. The sky is blue, but the vegetation and tree do not fully match.</figcaption>
</figure>
<h2>1. Keep the lightness, predict the colour</h2>
<p>Start with a pixel in the sky. Rather than ask the model to predict its red, green and blue values from scratch, I separate lightness from colour using the CIE L*a*b* colour space.</p>
<p><strong>L*</strong> represents lightness. <strong>a*</strong> runs roughly from green to red, and <strong>b*</strong> from blue to yellow. My input is L*. The target is the pair (a*, b*). At the end, I combine the predicted pair with the original L*, then convert back to RGB for display.</p>

<p>The notebook also scales the values before training: L*/50 - 1 for the input, and a*/128 and b*/128 for the targets. Those are numerical conventions, not new colours. Reconstruction reverses the scaling before converting to RGB.</p>
<p>This split gives the model a narrower job. The sky’s shape and lightness are already there; the model has to estimate its missing colour. But to do that, it first needs clues about what the pixel belongs to.</p>
<h2>2. Detail is useful, but context changes its meaning</h2>
<p>A convolution is a small filter moved across an image. It can respond to patterns such as edges and textures. Stacking these layers lets the encoder build a more useful representation than individual pixel values.</p>
<figure class="fig color-viz">
<div class="viz-row viz-row4">
<div><img src="/assets/images/projects/colorization/ex1-gray.png" alt="Grayscale landscape input"><span>Input L*</span></div>
<div><img src="/assets/images/projects/colorization/filter-vertical-edges.png" alt="Vertical edge response: bright along rock cracks and the cliff edge"><span>vertical edges</span></div>
<div><img src="/assets/images/projects/colorization/filter-horizontal-edges.png" alt="Horizontal edge response: bright along the tree line and cloud bottoms"><span>horizontal edges</span></div>
<div><img src="/assets/images/projects/colorization/filter-local-contrast.png" alt="Local contrast: texture on rock and trees, flat sky"><span>local contrast</span></div>
</div>
<figcaption><strong>Illustration, not the model's weights.</strong> I did not keep trained filters from the project, so these three maps come from hand-made Sobel and local-contrast filters applied to the real grayscale input. A trained first layer learns its own filters, but it responds to the same kind of thing: rock texture lights up, flat sky does not.</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>A smooth light patch, though, could be sky, a wall or fabric. Its nearby pixels help, but so does knowing whether the entire image looks like an outdoor landscape or an indoor room. That is why my model has a second path.</p>
<p>I send a grayscale version of the image to Inception-ResNet-v2, pretrained on ImageNet. Its expected input is 299 × 299 × 3, so I resize and repeat grayscale across three channels. <strong>Repeating grayscale does not restore colour.</strong> It only makes the input compatible with the pretrained network.</p>

<p>The project report calls the pretrained representation high-level features. There is a useful implementation detail here: the v2 notebook creates Inception-ResNet-v2 with <code>include_top=True</code> and uses <code>inception.predict</code>. That gives the 1,000 class outputs. The report describes a vector before softmax, so its wording and this notebook are not identical. So in this post I just call it a <strong>1,000-value pretrained representation</strong>.</p>
<h2>3. Put the scene context at every location</h2>
<p>Now there are two things of different shapes. The encoder produces a grid of local features. The pretrained network produces one vector for the image. Fusion repeats that vector at every position in the grid and concatenates it with the local features.</p>
<p>In the v2 notebook the input is 256 × 256. Three stride-2 convolutions reduce height and width by a factor of eight, so the encoder output is 32 × 32 × 256. Repeating the 1,000-value context vector over that grid produces 32 × 32 × 1,000. Concatenating them gives 32 × 32 × 1,256. (The report used a 128 × 128 input, which gives a 16 × 16 grid with the same channel counts.)</p>

<p>A 1 × 1 convolution does not look across neighbouring positions. It learns how to combine channels at the current position. After that, the decoder uses convolutions and upsampling to produce two colour values at each output pixel.</p>
<p>For my sky pixel, the path is now complete: local features describe its region, scene features provide context, and the decoder predicts (a*, b*). Training tells the model how close that guess came to the original.</p>

<h2>What the model can and can’t do</h2>
<p>The output examples are a fair place to end. The model can put blue into sky and green into grass, while missing a shirt’s actual colour. Local detail and scene context help make a guess; they do not turn that guess into a record of what was there.</p>
<p>I reproduced an approach and got a small model working. I didn’t show that it generalises well, and I never compared it against a baseline. What I took away is to keep milestones apart when describing a project like this: fitting one image, producing one plausible example and handling unseen images reliably are three very different claims.</p>
