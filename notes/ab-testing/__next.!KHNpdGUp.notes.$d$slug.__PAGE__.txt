2d:T7a2,<p>Random assignment creates comparable groups; a shared metric defines what is compared. A p-value describes how surprising the data would be under a specified null hypothesis. It is not the probability that the hypothesis is true. Decide the measurement and testing plan before interpreting a favourable result.</p>
<p>Before launch, write down:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Unit of randomization</strong> - user, session or request. The unit decides both the analysis and what carryover effects you are exposed to.</li>
<li><strong>Primary metric and guardrails</strong> - one metric decides, a few guardrails (latency, errors, revenue) stop you shipping a win that breaks something else.</li>
<li><strong>MDE and duration</strong> - the smallest effect worth acting on sets the sample size; run full weeks so weekday mix is balanced, and do not stop early because the curve looks good. Peeking without a sequential correction inflates false positives.</li>
</ul>
<p>Gotchas: novelty effects fade, so a first-week lift is not a steady-state lift; ratio metrics need a delta method or bootstrap, not a plain t-test on user ratios; and with twenty metrics something is always “significant” - pre-register the one that counts.</p>
<p>Check before reading any result: a <strong>sample ratio mismatch</strong> (a 50/50 split that came out 51/49 on a big sample, chi-square p &#x3C; 0.001) means assignment or logging is broken, and no metric from that test can be trusted. Analyse users at <strong>exposure</strong> (when they actually saw the variant), not at assignment, or unexposed users dilute the effect. Never change a live experiment’s salt, choice order or weights - hashing only repeats the same answer while the definition stays the same.</p>
<p>Read the full example: <a href="/posts/p-values-and-statistical-tests/">related post</a>.
